Exercise 1 — Modelling a phenomenon with an agent

Begun in Section 1 and due Friday 11 September at 11:59 pm. Graded on completion. You are not expected to finish it in the room.

What you are doing¶

  1. Onboarding. Get your agent running.

  2. Prompting. Pick one of the three phenomena below and frame it as a modelling problem for your agent.

  3. Investigating. Look at what it produced.

  4. Interpreting. Explain your model and its assumptions to your group, and compare the three.

  5. Interrogating. Go back over the model and work out what it assumed, what mathematics it uses, and whether you believe it.

The first four happen in section. Aim to get through Interpreting before we stop for the quiz. All of the writing is done afterwards, and the questions on Ed are divided into these same parts, so you always know which bit of the session you are being asked about.

An agent will do the modelling. Deciding what to ask it, judging what it produces, and identifying what you do not understand are left to you. Those are the parts this exercise is about.

Where the writing goes

Exercise 1 on Ed, after section. Nothing is handed in during section and there is nothing to upload.

Onboarding¶

You were asked to arrive with an agent working (setup guide). If you did, help the others in your group. If yours will not run, say so at the start rather than at the end. You will pair up and do the typing yourself. Save or email yourself whatever comes out before you leave, since it will be on somebody else’s laptop and you will need it on Friday.

Prompting¶

Groups of three, or two. Each student picks a different phenomenon, so between you the group covers all three. All three came from what this class submitted on Exercise 0.

A · Job applications
B · Calling the market
C · A production line

A graduating senior sends nearly the same résumé to a long list of postings. A few employers reply with an invitation to a first-round interview, most send a rejection, and some never answer. People count different things when they describe how it went: replies, interviews, or offers. Callback rates vary by field and by year, and two people applying to the same jobs can see very different results. University career offices publish callback and placement rates from their own surveys, and some job platforms collect application outcomes too. Advice about how many applications to send circulates as round numbers, and some applicants report sending several hundred without a single interview.

A stock index finishes each trading day above, below, or level with its previous close. Across long stretches of U.S. market history, up days have been somewhat more common than down days. Newsletter writers, fund managers and anonymous accounts publish calls on the market’s direction, over horizons that run from the next close to the next year. Some of them have long runs of correct calls behind them, and those runs are what the marketing quotes. Daily closing prices for the major indexes are free and go back decades, so a call written down in advance can be checked later against what happened.

A supply contract states limits for a part, and it may also require that at least 99% of parts fall inside them. Lines drift as tools wear and materials change. Inspecting every part is often impractical, so a company checks a sample from each batch and decides on that basis whether to accept the batch, hold it, or send it back. Both errors are expensive: rejecting a good batch holds up production, and accepting a bad one ships defects to a customer. A line that is meeting its target will still produce the occasional sample that looks bad, and a line that has started to drift will still produce samples that look fine. How many parts get inspected, and what result triggers a rejection, are set by the contract, by industry standards, and by what the defects would cost.

Frame it as a modelling problem to your agent, in a prompt of your own. You can leave it open and see what the agent does with it. You can also try constraining it, by making explicit assumptions or by focusing on the particular aspects of the phenomenon that interest you. Either is a reasonable thing to do.

Whatever else you ask for, ask for these:

  • an explanation of its approach, and a justification for it;

  • its assumptions, made explicit rather than left in the code;

  • artifacts: markdown documents, runnable code, etc.

Investigating¶

Look at what the agent actually produced, and run the code if there is code. Open the files rather than taking its word for what is in them. Some agents write into the folder you opened and some write somewhere else and tell you the path, so read to the end of the answer before you conclude it made nothing. Keep the conversation open, since you will need it on Friday.

Interpreting¶

About a minute each. Explain your model and its assumptions to the others in your group, without code and without formulas.

Then take note of any similarities in the models of the three phenomena. Are there common structures or assumptions? Or do they differ, and if so, how?

Get this far before we stop for the quiz.

Interrogating — after section¶

The questions on Ed ask what your model assumed without being told to, what mathematics it uses, where any of its numbers came from, and how you would ‘validate’ it. AM215 students have one more, on how you would ‘verify’ the code. Then a few general questions about how the session went, and a last short set looking ahead to next week.

The boxes are short and there is one per thing asked. Due Friday at 11:59 pm, and none of it needs a working agent.
