Chapter 10: Epidemiological Models

This chapter compares the infection and removal rules of mechanistic SIR with autoregressive forecasts that update predicted counts from recent observations, and shows how Rt=βS(t)/γR_t=\beta S(t)/\gammaRt​=βS(t)/γ determines whether infections grow while immunity above 1−1/R01-1/R_01−1/R0​ makes a small introduction decline.

Motivation¶

An epidemic is a spreading phenomenon: individuals have states, and interactions can change those states. Suppose one infectious person enters a population in which almost everyone can catch the disease. Will infections grow? If so, when will they stop growing? A mechanistic model starts with rules for those state changes and derives an epidemic curve. We will look at the most famous of such models: the SIR model, introduced by Kermack and McKendrick in 1927.

A statistical model can use observations directly to predict the near-term future, without specifying how an infection passes between people. We will study linear and exponential autoregressive forecast rules that update each predicted count from its preceding value.

SIR has three state variables and two parameters, yet it gives a threshold for initial growth, shows that the growth is exponential, and shows that infections later naturally decline as each infection reduces the number still susceptible. The basic reproduction number R0=βN/γR_0=\beta N/\gammaR0​=βN/γ summarizes the early behavior and provides a threshold for exponential growth.

The statistical forecasts have no analogous susceptible compartment or reproduction number. Their parameters describe a pattern in observations, but they can be extremely effective and efficient for short-term accurate predictions.

Two classes of models¶

  • Mechanistic models specify the states and the events that change them. Their equations express assumptions about transmission and removal. A compartmental SIR model is one example.

  • Statistical models estimate a relationship from observed counts and use it to forecast later counts. An autoregressive rule uses earlier values of a series to predict its next value. It need not contain, or be derived from, SIR compartments.

A model may combine both ideas, for example by fitting a mechanistic transmission process to noisy reported counts. Here we study each class on its own so that the meaning of its parameters and predictions is clear.

Mechanistic models: SIR¶

Compartments and notation¶

A compartmental model describes a population of interacting individuals under three assumptions:

  • every individual is in exactly one state, its compartment, and people in the same compartment have the same modeled behavior;

  • changes of state (i.e. of compartment) follow fixed transition rules;

  • the population is well mixed: a susceptible person’s chance of meeting an infectious person depends on the infectious count, rather than on a contact network. We assume the infection rate follows the mass-action law, proportional to the product of the susceptible and infectious counts.

SIR has three compartments, and at any instant:

  • S(t)S(t)S(t) — number of susceptible individuals.

  • I(t)I(t)I(t) — number of infected (and infectious) individuals.

  • R(t)R(t)R(t) — number of removed individuals (recovered with immunity, or dead).

We assume a closed population: S+I+R=NS + I + R = NS+I+R=N (constant). New symbols:

  • β\betaβ — transmission rate: the rate at which susceptible-infected contacts produce new infections. Units: (individuals)−1⋅time−1\text{(individuals)}^{-1} \cdot \text{time}^{-1}(individuals)−1⋅time−1.

  • γ\gammaγ — removal rate: the rate at which infectious individuals leave III. Units: time−1\text{time}^{-1}time−1. In the corresponding stochastic model, the mean infectious period is 1/γ1/\gamma1/γ.

  • R0=βN/γR_0 = \beta N / \gammaR0​=βN/γ — basic reproduction number: average number of secondary infections from a single infected case in a fully-susceptible population. Dimensionless.

The compartment R(t)R(t)R(t) and the reproduction number R0R_0R0​ are different quantities despite their similar notation.

From transition rules to the SIR equations¶

The rules are two reactions, S→I→RS \to I \to RS→I→R:

  1. Infection, S+I→2IS + I \to 2IS+I→2I. It needs a susceptible and an infectious person to meet. By mass action the rate is proportional to the number of such pairs: βSI\beta S IβSI new infections per unit time.

  2. Removal, I→RI \to RI→R. It involves one person and no meeting, so the rate is γI\gamma IγI removals per unit time.

Units check: βSI\beta S IβSI is a number of individuals per unit time, and SIS ISI is individuals squared, so β\betaβ has units of (individuals)−1⋅time−1\text{(individuals)}^{-1} \cdot \text{time}^{-1}(individuals)−1⋅time−1, as stated in the setup. γ\gammaγ is a plain rate time−1\text{time}^{-1}time−1.

When SSS people remain susceptible, each infectious person causes infections at rate βS\beta SβS and leaves the infectious compartment at rate γ\gammaγ. Removed people do not become susceptible again in this model.

The basic reproduction number R0R_0R0​¶

  • R0>1R_0 > 1R0​>1: a small introduction initially grows exponentially.

Aside: estimating R0R_0R0​. One can fit a transmission model to case counts, but those counts depend on testing, reporting, and the delay between infection and observation. Estimating R0R_0R0​ also needs, beyond the initial slope, an infectious-period assumption or information about the early level and reporting of infections. An estimate therefore belongs to a specified population, period, and model, rather than to a disease in every setting.

Herd immunity threshold¶

How many people must be immune in advance for a small infection to decline? Infection grows while Rt=R0S/N>1R_t=R_0S/N>1Rt​=R0​S/N>1. Worked example 1: numerical integration¶

This cell integrates a population of one million with one initial infectious person and prints the peak and final removed fraction:

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import odeint

def sir_rhs(y, t, beta, gamma):
    S, I, R = y
    dS = -beta * S * I
    dI =  beta * S * I - gamma * I
    dR =  gamma * I
    return [dS, dI, dR]

N = 1_000_000
I0 = 1
S0 = N - I0
R0_compartment = 0
beta, gamma = 0.3 / N, 0.1     # so R0 = beta * N / gamma = 3
t = np.linspace(0, 200, 1000)
sol = odeint(sir_rhs, [S0, I0, R0_compartment], t, args=(beta, gamma))
S, I, R = sol.T
print(f"Peak infections: {I.max():.0f} at day {t[I.argmax()]:.1f}")
print(f"Final attack rate: {R[-1]/N:.2%}")

The peak infectious count is about 300,000 near day 73, and the final removed fraction is about 94%. Early I(t)I(t)I(t) follows an exponential approximation; after SSS crosses N/R0N/R_0N/R0​, III declines. Check that S+I+R=NS+I+R=NS+I+R=N at each output time: every infection and removal must transfer a person between compartments. This checks the implementation, while comparison with observed data would test whether its assumptions describe an outbreak. The full version in code/10_sir_epidemiology/sir_basic.py plots all three compartments and marks the peak threshold.

Worked example 2: fitting SIR to daily case counts¶

Suppose we observe the number of new infections each day, rather than the cumulative total. For a one-day interval [t,t+1][t,t+1][t,t+1], the SIR prediction is S(t)−S(t+1)S(t)-S(t+1)S(t)−S(t+1). We generate independent Poisson counts around those daily predictions and fit βN\beta NβN and γ\gammaγ by maximum likelihood. The population size and initial infectious count are treated as known so that we can focus on the two rates.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import odeint
from scipy.optimize import minimize

N, I0 = 100_000, 5
days = np.arange(101)

def sir_rhs(y, t, beta, gamma):
    S, I, R = y
    return [-beta * S * I, beta * S * I - gamma * I, gamma * I]

def expected_cases(rates, time_grid):
    transmission, gamma = rates   # transmission = beta * N
    sol = odeint(sir_rhs, [N - I0, I0, 0], time_grid,
                 args=(transmission / N, gamma))
    return np.maximum(sol[:-1, 0] - sol[1:, 0], 1e-12)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
observed = rng.poisson(expected_cases([0.35, 0.15], days))

def fit(day_count):
    grid = days[:day_count + 1]
    counts = observed[:day_count]
    def nll(rates):
        predicted = expected_cases(rates, grid)
        return np.sum(predicted - counts * np.log(predicted))
    result = minimize(nll, [0.3, 0.1], method="L-BFGS-B",
                      bounds=[(1e-4, 2.0), (1e-4, 2.0)])
    assert result.success
    transmission, gamma = result.x
    return transmission, gamma, transmission / gamma

for label, rates in [("true", (0.35, 0.15, 0.35 / 0.15)),
                     ("first 25 days", fit(25)), ("all 100 days", fit(100))]:
    print(f"{label}: beta*N={rates[0]:.3f}, gamma={rates[1]:.3f}, R0={rates[2]:.3f}")

The first 25 days give R^0=2.789\widehat R_0=2.789R0​=2.789, while the full 100 days give 2.327, close to the true value 2.333. The short-window estimate is sensitive to noise and the assumption that I0I_0I0​ is known. This likelihood uses daily counts because the Poisson draws in the example are independent conditional on the deterministic curve. Cumulative counts reuse earlier observations, so treating each cumulative total as an independent Poisson observation would count the same cases repeatedly. A real data analysis would also model reporting and observation delays.

The early exponential slope constrains r=βN−γr=\beta N-\gammar=βN−γ. The level of daily incidence supplies another constraint on βN\beta NβN here because I0I_0I0​ and reporting are assumed known. If either is unknown, that level can be rescaled, leaving many pairs (β,γ)(\beta,\gamma)(β,γ) with the same slope. Later observations of the curve bending and declining help separate the rates. The fit on simulated data tests parameter recovery under our assumed observation model; it does not validate that observation model for real cases. The longer companion script is in code/10_sir_epidemiology/sir_fit.py.

Statistical models: autoregressive forecasts¶

Predicting from recent observations¶

The SIR fit required a transmission model to account for observed counts. We can also start with an observed series, such as cumulative reported death counts D(t)D(t)D(t), and extend its recent trend. Deaths have reporting delays and noise but can be less affected by case ascertainment than reported infections. The choice of series, its reporting process, and the forecast horizon still matter.

An autoregressive forecast updates the next value from earlier values of the same series. For equally spaced observations, a general rule has the form

X^(t+1)=f(X(t),X(t−1),…).\widehat X(t+1)=f\big(X(t),X(t-1),\ldots\big).X(t+1)=f(X(t),X(t−1),…).
(9)

For a forecast beyond one step, predicted values replace future observations as inputs. The linear and exponential rules below fit their coefficients on a recent time window; each then updates its own predicted value through a lag-one recurrence.

Linear Model. Let t=1t=1t=1 denote the first day in a selected window. Fit a linear trend to its death counts, writing the fitted value as D^(t)=b0+b1(t−1)\widehat D(t)=b_0+b_1(t-1)D(t)=b0​+b1​(t−1). Advancing one day and subtracting gives

D^(t+1)−D^(t)=b1,orD^(t+1)=D^(t)+b1.\widehat D(t+1)-\widehat D(t)=b_1, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \boxed{\widehat D(t+1)=\widehat D(t)+b_1.}D(t+1)−D(t)=b1​,orD(t+1)=D(t)+b1​.​
(10)

Thus the forecast adds the same absolute increment each day, starting from D^(1)=b0\widehat D(1)=b_0D(1)=b0​. This is a lag-one autoregressive update of predicted values with coefficient one and intercept b1b_1b1​.

Exponential Model. We can also fit a line to the logarithms instead: log⁡D^(t)=e0+e1(t−1)\log\widehat D(t)=e_0+e_1(t-1)logD(t)=e0​+e1​(t−1), or D^(t)=exp⁡[e0+e1(t−1)]\widehat D(t)=\exp[e_0+e_1(t-1)]D(t)=exp[e0​+e1​(t−1)]. The daily difference on the log scale is e1e_1e1​, so

log⁡D^(t+1)−log⁡D^(t)=e1,orD^(t+1)=ee1D^(t).\log\widehat D(t+1)-\log\widehat D(t)=e_1, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \boxed{\widehat D(t+1)=e^{e_1}\widehat D(t).}logD(t+1)−logD(t)=e1​,orD(t+1)=ee1​D(t).​
(11)

This forecast has a constant multiplicative factor ee1e^{e_1}ee1​ per day, starting from D^(1)=ee0\widehat D(1)=e^{e_0}D(1)=ee0​. The coefficients bib_ibi​ and eie_iei​ describe fitted trends and they are different from the transmission rate β\betaβ.

The two rules can be refitted as new observations arrive, then iterated from the end of the fitting window for short forecasts. They contain no susceptible depletion, so neither can anticipate a change in epidemic phase from its fixed update rule. Linear and exponential components were used in county-level COVID-19 death forecasting by Altieri et al. (Further reading). In the next example we apply the same mathematics to cumulative infections C(t)C(t)C(t).

Worked example 3: extrapolating short-window trends¶

How well does each regression predict ten days beyond a ten-day window? We use the cumulative count C=I+RC=I+RC=I+R generated by SIR so we know the future values against which to check the forecasts. The cell fits a line to CCC and another to log⁡C\log ClogC, once during early growth and once near the peak:

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import odeint

def sir_rhs(y, t, beta, gamma):
    S, I, R = y
    return [-beta * S * I, beta * S * I - gamma * I, gamma * I]

N, I0 = 1_000_000, 1
beta, gamma = 0.3 / N, 0.1
days = np.arange(0, 121)
sol = odeint(sir_rhs, [N - I0, I0, 0], days, args=(beta, gamma))
C = sol[:, 1] + sol[:, 2]

for start in [20, 65]:
    window = np.arange(start, start + 10)
    b1, b0 = np.polyfit(days[window], C[window], 1)
    e1, e0 = np.polyfit(days[window], np.log(C[window]), 1)
    t10 = days[start + 19]
    print(f"day {t10}: true {C[t10]:.0f}, linear {b0 + b1 * t10:.0f}, "
          f"exponential {np.exp(e0 + e1 * t10):.0f}")

In the early window (days 20–29), the fitted exponential daily multiplier is ee1=1.222e^{e_1}=1.222ee1​=1.222, close to er=e0.2e^r=e^{0.2}er=e0.2. At day 39 the true cumulative count is 3,644; the exponential forecast is 3,675 and the linear forecast is 879. For the window on days 65–74, the day-84 count is 860,655, while the linear and exponential forecasts are 1,081,996 and 1,490,685. Both now overshoot because their fixed update rules do not anticipate the bending of the cumulative curve. The exponential model works well for the early ten-day forecast and poorly for the later one. Intuition¶

If R0>1R_0>1R0​>1, why can infections be declining today? R0R_0R0​ describes transmission in a fully susceptible population under specified conditions. At time ttt, SIR uses Rt=R0S(t)/NR_t=R_0S(t)/NRt​=R0​S(t)/N. Prior infections or vaccination can reduce SSS until Rt<1R_t<1Rt​<1, even though R0R_0R0​ remains above 1. The sign of I˙\dot II˙ depends on the current susceptible count, not on R0R_0R0​ alone.

Is the herd immunity threshold the fraction eventually infected? No. The threshold 1−1/R01-1/R_01−1/R0​ is the immune fraction at which a small new introduction stops growing in homogeneous SIR. During an outbreak, crossing S=N/R0S=N/R_0S=N/R0​ only marks the peak of I(t)I(t)I(t): people already infectious continue to cause new cases. With R0=3R_0=3R0​=3, the peak occurs when roughly 67% have been infected, but the final-size equation gives roughly 94%. The difference is the overshoot described above.

Can the early growth rate determine R0R_0R0​? Near the disease-free state, I(t)≈I0ertI(t)\approx I_0e^{rt}I(t)≈I0​ert with r=βN−γr=\beta N-\gammar=βN−γ. Many pairs of β\betaβ and γ\gammaγ give the same rrr but different R0=βN/γR_0=\beta N/\gammaR0​=βN/γ. In Worked example 2, the level of daily incidence adds information because I0I_0I0​ and reporting are assumed known. If either is uncertain, the same early slope supports more than one reproduction-number estimate.

How do the two statistical updates differ? The linear rule adds the fitted increment b1b_1b1​ each day; the exponential rule multiplies by ee1e^{e_1}ee1​ each day. Both update a forecast of the observed series, and neither coefficient is the transmission rate β\betaβ. In the early window of Worked example 3, the exponential multiplier agrees with the SIR growth approximation. Later, susceptible depletion changes the trend. At day 84 both forecasts exceed the population of one million, which a cumulative infection count cannot do.

Solutions

1. R0R_0R0​ is an average, so individuals can cause different numbers of infections. It refers to the fully susceptible limit; later in SIR the current value is Rt=R0S/NR_t=R_0S/NRt​=R0​S/N. It also depends on the contact conditions used to define β\betaβ, which need not stay fixed. None of these facts supports “exactly three others throughout.”

2. Initially S/N≈1−vS/N\approx1-vS/N≈1−v, so Rt≈R0(1−v)R_t\approx R_0(1-v)Rt​≈R0​(1−v). The infectious count declines when Rt<1R_t<1Rt​<1, giving v>1−1/R0v>1-1/R_0v>1−1/R0​. For R0=3R_0=3R0​=3 this means more than two thirds of the population must be immune; at exactly two thirds the initial growth rate is zero in the approximation. In an unmitigated epidemic with R0=3R_0=3R0​=3, infections continue after the peak and the final attack rate is about 94%, rather than 67%. The threshold concerns initial growth after immunity is present; the final size concerns the entire epidemic.

7. R0R_0R0​ refers to the fully susceptible limit under specified transmission conditions, while RtR_tRt​ includes the current susceptible fraction.
